How to read the MT5 Strategy Tester report

An MT5 backtest report has dozens of figures, and almost everything important is in a handful of them. This guide explains what each one means and how MT5 calculates it, with worked examples, what values are reasonable, which ones should make you suspicious and what to check about the test conditions before trusting any of them.

In short

  1. Net profit and profit factor: whether it makes money, and by what margin.
  2. Drawdown: the worst drop, in money and in percentage, which can be two different episodes.
  3. Recovery factor, expected payoff and Sharpe ratio: how much it earns per unit of risk.
  4. Number of trades: whether there are enough to conclude anything.
  5. The test conditions: modeling, history quality and costs decide whether the rest is believable.

How to get the report

The report belongs to a single test, not to an optimization. To get the report of a configuration, set its values in the Inputs tab (or load its .set), choose “Disabled” in the Optimization field of the Settings tab and run the test. When it finishes, the Backtest tab shows the report; right-click it and choose Report to save it as HTML or Open XML.

Net profit and profit factor

Gross Profit is the sum of all winning trades and Gross Loss, the sum of all losing ones (shown as a negative number). Total Net Profit is the two added together. Profit Factor is gross profit divided by gross loss in absolute value: 1 means it makes nothing, 1.5 means it wins 1.5 for every unit it loses.

Example. Gross profit 15,000 and gross loss −10,000: net profit 5,000 and profit factor 15,000 / 10,000 = 1.5.

As a rule of thumb, not a law, a profit factor around 1.3–1.5 over many trades is often considered reasonable. Values of 3 or more over many trades are rare and deserve suspicion, especially when they come out of an optimization. With few trades, the profit factor swings wildly: a couple of losing trades turned into winners can change it drastically.

Drawdown: absolute, maximal and relative

MT5 gives three drawdowns, each one for the balance (closed trades only) and for the equity (including the open ones):

  • Absolute: how far the account fell below the initial deposit. If it never went below it, it is zero.
  • Maximal: the largest drop in money from a local peak to the next low, with that same drop as a percentage of its peak in brackets.
  • Relative: the largest drop as a percentage of its peak, with that drop in money in brackets.

The maximal and the relative are two separate maxima, and they can come from different episodes: the largest drop in money is not necessarily the largest in percentage.

Example. The balance goes from 10,000 down to 7,000, then up to 20,000 and down to 16,000. The first drop is 3,000 (30% of its peak); the second, 4,000 (20%). The report shows a maximal drawdown of 4,000 (20%) and a relative drawdown of 30% (3,000).

Look at the equity figures: the balance only moves when a trade closes, so it can hide trades that went deep against you before closing. Then ask yourself whether you would sit through that drop with real money, because the next one can be larger.

Recovery factor

Recovery Factor is net profit divided by the maximal drawdown. With a net profit of 5,000 and a maximal drawdown of 2,500, it would be 2: you earned twice what you lost at the worst moment. Higher is better.

Compare it only between tests of the same length: profit tends to grow in proportion to time while the worst drop grows more slowly, so a longer test usually shows a higher recovery factor without the strategy being any better.

Expected payoff

Expected Payoff is net profit divided by the number of trades: what each trade leaves on average. With 5,000 of net profit over 250 trades, it is 20 per trade.

Compare it with your cost per trade. If your lot makes each point worth 1, those 20 are 20 points per trade; a broker with a spread one or two points wider, or a little slippage, takes a visible part of it. If the expected payoff is only a few points, a slightly worse execution eats it whole.

Sharpe ratio

The Sharpe Ratio measures return relative to how irregular it is: the average return divided by its standard deviation, with a risk-free rate of zero in the tester. Since build 3210 (2022), MT5 calculates it on the equity, including the swings of open trades, and scales it to a one-year interval.

MetaQuotes gives these bands: below 0 the strategy loses; between 0 and 1 the risk does not pay off; from 1 it is considered good; from 3, very good. Over short tests the figure is noisy, so use it above all to compare configurations tested over the same period.

Number of trades and how the profit is spread

Total Trades is the most ignored figure and the one that decides whether the others mean anything. With 30 trades, a couple of lucky wins change the profit factor completely; with 300, much less. There is no universal minimum, but below about 100 over the whole period it pays to be very cautious, even more so if the configuration came out of an optimization that tried thousands of combinations.

Then look at how the profit is spread. The report gives the largest profit trade and the maximum consecutive losses, among others. If the largest trade is 1,800 out of a net profit of 5,000, more than a third of the result comes from a single trade: take away the two or three best ones and there may be nothing left. A robust edge is spread across many trades.

The test conditions

Before believing any figure, check how the test was run. The top of the report shows the settings and the History Quality: according to the MT5 help, the share of correct one-minute data in the history used, where gaps and malformed bars count as incorrect. A figure well below 100% means part of the history was missing or faulty.

  • Every tick based on real ticks: uses your broker’s real tick history, with its real spread. It is the most faithful mode and the slowest.
  • Every tick: generates ticks from one-minute bars. Faster, but the moves inside each minute are simulated.
  • 1 minute OHLC: only the open, high, low and close of each one-minute bar. Fast, fine for exploring.
  • Open prices only: only the opening price of each bar. It only makes sense if the EA makes its decisions at the opening of a new bar.

Also check the costs: if the report shows no commission and your broker charges one, or the spread of the test is narrower than what you get live, the backtest is optimistic. The Delays option in the settings simulates execution latency, including a random delay.

Traps when reading the report

  • Looking only at net profit. Without drawdown and number of trades, it says nothing about risk or about whether it is chance.
  • Comparing reports with different periods or deposits. Profit and drawdown in money depend on both; percentages and ratios travel better.
  • Trusting a test with poor modeling or poor history. Check the modeling mode and the history quality before anything else.
  • Reading the report of the best pass of an optimization as if it were a clean test. That pass was chosen precisely because it did well in that period; only a period you did not use to choose it is a clean test.
